Abstract— Building footprint extraction (BFE) is essential for
applications like land use management, urban planning, and
database updates. However, many existing methods struggle with
occluded buildings in remote sensing images, causing perfor-
mance degradation. In addition, methods designed for occluded
buildings often depend on external data, such as LiDAR or
multiview images, increasing extraction costs. To address this
challenge, we present a robust building extraction framework
that effectively targets occluded buildings. Specifically, we design
a low-cost image enhancement strategy. This strategy leverages
the segmented labels to introduce object information about the
buildings, aiding in generating enhanced images with strong
building integrity, no occlusions, and high contrast with the
background. Then, a building integrity enhancement branch
(BIEB) is proposed. This branch incorporates enhanced building
images as supervision, in order to leverage building integrity
to mitigate the impact of occlusion. Finally, we introduce the
idea of residual learning. This approach facilitates the model’s
ability to leverage building integrity to mitigate the impact of
occlusion. Extensive experiments on the WHU, Massachusetts,
Inria, and Aerial Imagery for Roof Segmentation (AIRS) datasets
have demonstrated the effectiveness of our approach in extract-
ing occluded buildings. The experimental results show that,
compared with the latest HD-Net, our BIENet achieved IoU
score improvements of 1.49%, 1.16%, and 2.26%, and F1 score
improvements of 0.82%, 0.70%, and 1.22%, respectively, on the
WHU, Inria, and AIRS datasets. The source code of the proposed
BIENet is available at https://github.com/tqwhdx19/bienet.
Index Terms— Building footprint extraction (BFE), image
integrity enhancement, occlusion, semantic segmentation.

and long-range global information at various stages within
the encoder network. However, these approaches still face
significant challenges in effectively addressing the issue of

To tackle the challenge of occluded buildings, several BFE
methods have been proposed. Zhu et al. [27] adopted oblique
images to obtain the 3-D structure of buildings from 2-D
footprints, and then proposed a fused feature pyramid network
using both color and depth information to reduce the impact
of occlusion. Maolin et al. [28] utilized aerial LiDAR point

an improved minimum bounding rectangle strategy specif-

methods heavily depend on external data, such as LiDAR
and multiview data, making them unsuitable for normal aerial

To overcome the aforementioned challenges, we introduce a
bi-branch integrity-enhanced network, termed BIENet, which
can achieve robust identification of occluded buildings in
a low-cost manner (see Fig. 1). Specifically, we propose a
low-cost image enhancement strategy designed to improve
building integrity. This strategy leverages segmentation labels
to introduce object information about the buildings, generat-
ing enhanced images. These images exhibit robust building
integrity, are free from occlusions, and possess high contrast
with the background. Since the segmentation labels are derived
solely from the original dataset, there are no additional data
collection costs, making the method cost-effective. Then, the
enhanced images serve as supervisory signals for the building
integrity enhancement branch (BIEB) to introduce building
integrity information. This enables the model to assist in
extracting occluded buildings based on the structural integrity
information of the building when it is occluded. Finally,
considering that directly reconstructing an enhanced image
is a challenging task, we introduce the concept of residual
learning to capture the difference between the enhanced image
and the original image. This approach facilitates the learning
of the building’s structural integrity information. The overall
framework of the proposed BIENet is shown in Fig. 2.
To summarize, the main contributions of this article are as
follows.
1) We propose a BIENet, which exploits the integrity
of buildings to aid in identifying occluded buildings.
BIENet is cost-effective, as it utilizes only images and
segmentation labels.
2) We design a low-cost image enhancement strategy. This
strategy leverages the segmentation labels to introduce
object information about the buildings and generate
enhanced images. These enhanced images exhibit robust
building integrity, are devoid of occlusions, and possess
a high degree of contrast with the surrounding back-
ground.
3) We propose adding a BIEB to reconstruct the enhanced
image and introducing the concept of residual learning
to aid in learning-building integrity information. This
enables the model to assist in extracting occluded build-
ings based on the structural integrity information when
they are occluded.
We conduct extensive experiments on four diverse
real-world building footprint datasets. The results show that
our method achieves superior performance, surpassing state-
of-the-art (SOTA) CNN-based and transformer-based BEF
models.
II. R ELATED WORK
A. CNN-Based BFE
CNNs can achieve deep discriminative feature extraction
through layer-by-layer stacking. Thus, it attracts much atten-
tion in the field of BFE. Building extraction is a dense
prediction task and needs to identify the class of each
pixel. Long et al. [18] proposed the use of the FCN for
semantic segmentation. The FCN can take input of arbitrary
size and produce correspondingly sized output with efficient
inference and learning, greatly promoting the application
of convolutional neural networks in BFE [19], [20], [21].
The segmentation results predicted by the original FCN are
rough and have inaccurate boundaries. Therefore, a series
of encoder–decoder methods have been proposed [11], [22].
Models that are based on an encoder–decoder structure, such
as UNet [22] and SegNet [11], employ a symmetric decoder.
This decoder progressively restores the spatial resolution of
extracted features, ultimately achieving fine-resolution feature
representation. Furthermore, considering the large differences
in scale of ground objects, a series of multiscale feature
extraction strategies have been proposed, such as[13] and [29].
To adapt to the changes in scale, scene, and color of buildings,
different attention mechanisms [30], [31] have been introduced
into the building footprint field to adaptively extract dis-
criminative features. Besides, to achieve real-time processing
and analysis of large-scale remote sensing imagery, some
lightweight networks [5], [32] have been proposed to improve
the building extraction efficiency. Due to the heavy reliance on
convolution operations, these methods have limited capacity
for effective global information modeling. Different from these

B. ViT-Based BFE
The transformer, a neural network architecture that relies
primarily on attention mechanisms, was initially introduced
in [33] for the purpose of machine translation within the field
of natural language processing (NLP). The transformer has a
strong ability to model long-range dependencies. This property
is helpful in improving the recognizability of features, and
facilitating the widespread application of transformers in BFE.
The semantic segmentation transformer [16] uses an exclusive
transformer architecture to represent images as sequences
of patches, particularly designed for semantic segmentation
tasks. Considering the limitations of traditional transformer
models like the ViT in terms of low-resolution outputs, various
approaches have been introduced to address this issue. These
include models with a pyramid structure, such as the pyramid
ViT (PVT) [17], [34], Swin transformer [15], and so on.
Recent studies have incorporated transformers into building
extraction tasks [9], [26], [35]. Wang et al. [9] utilized a
dual-path approach that includes a global context path and
a spatial-detailed context path to effectively capture global
dependencies. Xu et al. [26], on the other hand, employed
two parallel branches consisting of multiscale CNNs and
transformers. This approach allows them to utilize both local
contextual details and global long-range information at every
encoder network stage. To leverage both local and global
contexts for building extraction, Zhang et al. [35] have devised
a dual spatial attention transformer network. This innovation
aims to optimize the strengths of both CNNs and ViTs. How-
ever, these transformer-based methods encounter difficulties in
precisely capturing the contours of buildings, particularly in
regions where trees or shadows obstruct the view. In com-
parison to existing transformer-based methods, the proposed
method initially produces enhanced images featuring strong
building integrity, no occlusion, and high contrast. These
enhanced images serve as a supervision signal, integrating the
building’s integrity into the model. This enables the model
to aid in extracting occluded buildings based on the integrity
structure information of the building, especially when the
building is partially hidden.
C. BFE With Occlusion
Partial occlusion in remote sensing occurs when objects like
buildings or roads are partially obscured by elements such
as trees, shadows, clouds, or other buildings, posing chal-
lenges for accurate data interpretation and analysis. Recently,
a small amount of work has been proposed to mitigate the
impact of occlusion on building extraction. Zhu et al. [27]
employed oblique images to derive the 3-D structure from
buildings’ 2-D footprints. They subsequently introduce a fused
feature pyramid network that incorporates both color and
depth information, aiming to mitigate the impact of occlusion.
Maolin et al. [28] leverages aerial LiDAR point cloud data to
incorporate 3-D information. They further devise an enhanced
minimum bounding rectangle strategy tailored to extract com-
prehensive and accurate regularized building boundaries, even
in the presence of partial occlusion. Nevertheless, these tech-
niques heavily rely on external sources of data, such as LiDAR
and multiview data, rendering them inapplicable for normal
aerial imagery datasets. Different from existing BFE methods
for occlusion, the proposed method leverages segmentation
labels to introduce object information about the buildings.
This approach generates enhanced images with robust building
integrity, no occlusions, and high contrast with the back-
ground. Because the segmentation labels only come from the
original dataset, there is no additional data collection cost. This
ensures that the method is low-cost and versatile.

A. Motivation and Overall Idea
Partial occlusion in remote sensing occurs when objects like
buildings are partially obscured by elements such as trees,
shadows, and clouds [27], [28], posing challenges for accurate
data interpretation and analysis. Fig. 3 provides some examples
of BFE with/without partial occlusion. Unfortunately, existing
BFE methods either ignore occlusion effects [23] or rely on
additional data, such as Lidar [28] or multiview data [27].
This motivates us to develop a framework that can achieve
robust identification of buildings with partial occlusion in a
low-cost manner. There are two main challenges for building
extraction with partial occlusion: 1) how to obtain the discrim-
inative features that are robust for occlusion and 2) from a
practical perspective, how to minimize costs while ensuring
effectiveness. Considering the low-cost requirement of the
solution, we only utilize the image and label data contained
in the original dataset without introducing any additional
data. Furthermore, we assume that buildings have structural
information and the structural information of buildings can be
used to estimate the occluded area. Considering that the seg-
mentation label contains the object information of buildings,
we utilize the segmentation label to introduce the building
integrity. Then, the introduced building integrity information
is used to generate enhanced images with strong building
integrity, no occlusion, and high contrast. These enhanced
images can serve as a supervision signal to integrate building
integrity into the model. This enables the model to assist in
the extraction of occluded buildings based on the structural
integrity information of the building.

IV. E XPERIMENTS
In this section, we first introduce the datasets used in our
study. Subsequently, we present the evaluation metrics and
experimental settings employed. Next, we comprehensively
compare our method with recent semantic segmentation and
BFE methods. Following this, we carefully analyze the role of
each component in ablation experiments. We then qualitatively
analyze the reasons why building integrity enhancement is
effective for partial occlusion. Finally, we analyze the effect
of building extraction with limited data.
A. Datasets
To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed BIENet,
experiments were conducted on four publicly available build-
ing datasets: the WHU building dataset, the Massachusetts
building dataset, the Inria aerial image labeling dataset, and the
aerial imagery for roof segmentation dataset. These datasets
encompass various scenarios involving different sensor types,
cities, resolutions, and scenes. The specifics of these datasets
are described below.
1) WHU Building Dataset: The aerial imagery dataset for the
WHU buildings [38] spans an area of more than 450 km 2,
containing approximately 22 000 buildings. The red-green-
blue (RGB) composite aerial images have a spatial resolution
of 0.3 m. These images, along with rasterized building labels,
are cropped into 8189 tiles, each with a resolution of 512 ×
512 pixels. Subsequently, the dataset is divided into three
distinct sets: training (consisting of 4736 tiles), validation
(consisting of 1036 tiles), and test (consisting of 2416 tiles).
We adhere to the original partition provided by the dataset to
ensure a fair evaluation.
2) Massachusetts Buildings Dataset: The dataset [39] com-
prises 151 aerial images, each captured in the Boston area and
spanning 1500 × 1500 pixels with a spatial resolution of 1 m.
These images provide a comprehensive representation of both
urban and suburban landscapes, showcasing a diverse range
of building sizes, shapes, textures, and colors. This diversity
makes the dataset an excellent benchmark for evaluating the
robustness of models across different scenes. To ensure a
rigorous evaluation, the dataset was divided into three separate
sets: a training set consisting of 137 tiles, a validation set with
4 tiles, and a test set with 10 tiles. We strictly adhered to this
partition and cropped the images as well as their corresponding
labels to 512 × 512 pixels, with an overlap rate of 0.5.
This approach generated 3076 tiles for training, 100 tiles for
validation, and 250 tiles for test.

is 0.15 m. The vector polygons outlining the buildings have
been manually adjusted to ensure precise alignment between
all building labels and their corresponding images. To improve
computational efficiency and enhance model training, the
images were cropped into smaller tiles of 512 × 512 pixels
with a 5-pixel overlap. In addition, any tiles within the training
set that did not contain any buildings were excluded. This
approach generated 116 080 tiles for training, 11 711 tiles for
validation, and 12 166 tiles for test.
B. Evaluation Metrics
To assess the effectiveness of the proposed methods, we use
four widely accepted evaluation metrics: intersection-over-
union (IoU), precision, recall, and F1 score. Precision gauges
the accuracy of predicted building areas, while recall measures
the proportion of correctly identified buildings in the ground
truth data. The F1 score offers a balanced assessment by
combining precision and recall through their harmonic mean.
The IoU calculates the overlap between the predicted building
areas and the ground truths, expressing the ratio of their
intersection to their union. Evaluation metrics involve counting
true positive (TP), false positive (FP), false negative (FN),
and true negative (TN) pixels, and can be expressed using
the following equations:
Precision = TP/(TP + FP) (8)
Recall = TP/(TP + FN) (9)
F1 = 2 × Precision × RecallPrecision + Recall (10)
IoU = TP/(TP + FP + FN). (11)

V. C ONCLUSION
In this article, we focus on the challenge of inaccurate
building extraction due to occlusions from nearby shadows
or trees. We propose a BIENet, which can achieve robust
identification of occluded buildings in a low-cost manner. The
image ESBI is designed to generate enhanced images with
strong building integrity, no occlusion, and high contrast with
the background. These enhanced images serve as a supervision
signal to introduce building integrity into the model via the
BIEB. This enables the model to utilize building integrity
information to assist in the identification of occluded buildings.
BRL enables the model to better utilize the structural integrity
information of the building to reduce the impact of occlusion.
From the extensive experimental results on multiple datasets,
we mainly conclude that: 1) building integrity can effec-
tively assist in the identification of obscured buildings and 2)
introducing expert knowledge through an image enhancement
method is helpful to improve the robustness of the model.
In the future, we intend to extend the proposed idea to other
dense prediction tasks, such as object detection in remote
sensing images.
