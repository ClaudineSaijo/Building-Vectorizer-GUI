ABSTRACT Structured building vectorization from high-resolution remote-sensing imagery provides
essential geometric representations for urban mapping, digital twin construction, and geographic information
systems (GIS). However, existing structured reconstruction approaches may still suffer from incomplete
vertex localization, unstable edge connectivity, and increased computational demands when processing
complex urban scenes. This article presents CornerDYO, an efficient corner–edge reasoning framework
for structured building vectorization from remote-sensing images. The proposed framework integrates
high-resolution feature enhancement, bidirectional feature fusion, context refinement, and corner-aware
attention to improve geometric representation and structural connectivity. Experiments are conducted on the
Outdoor Architecture Reconstruction benchmark introduced in previous structured reconstruction studies,
containing 2,001 satellite image patches from Paris, Las V egas, and Atlanta. Under the controlled evaluation
setting, CornerDYO achieves 98.5% precision, 95.0% recall, and 96.7% F1-score for corner detection at
512 × 512 resolution, outperforming HEA T by 8.0 percentage points in F1-score. For edge detection,
CornerDYO improves the F1-score from 79.7% to 83.8%. Moreover, under the same 500-epoch training
schedule, CornerDYO reduces the training time from 6 h 28 min to 2 h 22 min. These results demonstrate
that CornerDYO provides an accurate structural primitive extraction front end for remote-sensing building
vectorization, achieving improved geometric prediction performance with reduced training time under the
same experimental setting.

management, and geographic information systems (GIS).
Compared with raster masks, dense point clouds, or boundary

complete vertices, preserve spatial accuracy, and infer

Existing methods related to this task can be broadly
divided into three categories: mask-based building extrac-
tion [4], generic line or wireframe detection, and structured
building reconstruction. Mask-based approaches, such as
semantic segmentation and instance segmentation models,
can achieve strong region-level building extraction perfor-

boundary maps. To obtain vectorized polygons, additional
contour tracing, simplification, regularization, and topol-

with occlusion, weak roof texture, shadows, repetitive

Learning-based line and wireframe detection methods
provide more structured outputs than dense segmenta-
tion. Methods such as L-CNN, HAWP , and LETR have
advanced junction detection, line segment grouping, and
wireframe parsing. Nevertheless, generic line detection does
not directly guarantee valid roof topology. In building
vectorization, it is not sufficient to detect visually salient
line segments independently; the detected primitives must
support stable vertex ordering, reliable edge adjacency, and
downstream polygon formation. Missing a single structural
corner may remove all incident edges from the graph,
while redundant or incorrect edges may produce invalid
cycles.
Recent structured reconstruction frameworks, including
Conv-MPN [6], HEA T[7], and CornerFormer [8], explicitly
model corner–edge relationships and have demonstrated
promising results for architectural structure reconstruction.
Conv-MPN performs message passing over candidate
geometric primitives, HEA T introduces transformer-based
holistic edge attention, and CornerFormer enhances corner
representation for fine-grained corner–edge reasoning.
However, these methods may still face two limitations
when applied to large-scale high-resolution remote-
sensing imagery. First, message-passing or transformer-
heavy designs may introduce increased computational
demands. Second, polygon validity may still be affected
by missed keypoints, unstable edge connectivity, or post-
processing-dependent graph formation in complex rooftop
scenes.
To address these limitations, this article proposes Cor-
nerDYO, an efficient corner–edge reasoning framework
for structured building vectorization from remote-sensing
images. Unlike raster- or boundary-driven methods that infer
polygons indirectly, CornerDYO directly predicts corner
confidence and edge adjacency as structural primitives. The
predicted vertices and connections are assembled into a pla-
nar graph, which provides a compact front-end representation
for downstream polygon-oriented vectorization. The focus
of this article is therefore on improving corner–edge primitive
quality rather than claiming complete end-to-end polygon-
level reconstruction superiority.
The proposed framework is designed around three task-
specific considerations. First, roof corners are sparse, local,
and sensitive to downsampling. To enhance the fused
representation with fine-grained vertex cues, CornerDYO
introduces an additional 52 × 52 high-resolution feature
level together with the original 26 × 26 and 13 × 13
feature scales. Second, single-direction feature fusion may
weaken shallow geometric cues. A two-way feature pyra-
mid is therefore adopted to combine top-down semantic
propagation with bottom-up geometric aggregation. Third,
complex rooftop scenes often contain occlusions, weak
textures, shadows, and repetitive structures. A localized
Swin-based context refinement block and hybrid channel–
spatial attention are used to enhance corner-discriminative
features before corner confidence and edge adjacency
prediction.
Compared with HEA T, CornerDYO places greater empha-
sis on strengthening corner-sensitive feature representation
before edge-adjacency reasoning. Its architectural distinction
does not rely on any individual generic module in isolation;
rather, it lies in the task-specific integration of high-resolution
feature enhancement, bidirectional multi-scale fusion, local-
ized context refinement, and corner-aware attention within a
corner–edge reasoning pipeline.
The main contributions of this article are summarized as
follows:
• An efficient corner–edge reasoning framework is pro-
posed for structured building vectorization from remote-
sensing images. The framework explicitly predicts
vertex-level corner confidence and edge adjacency,
providing a structural primitive extraction front-end
for planar graph construction and polygon-oriented
vectorization.
• A vertex-preserving feature enhancement design is
developed by integrating CSPDarknet-Light, an addi-
tional high-resolution feature level, two-way feature
pyramid fusion, localized Swin-based context refine-
ment, and hybrid channel–spatial attention. This design
is tailored to strengthen sparse roof-junction representa-

• Experiments on the Outdoor Architecture Reconstruc-

both corner and edge F1-scores in the controlled
comparison with HEA T while reducing training wall-
clock time under the same 500-epoch schedule. Pro-
gressive component-integration and qualitative analyses
further illustrate how the integrated design contributes
to corner completeness, edge continuity, and graph-
oriented structural reliability.

II. RELATED WORK
A. BUILDING EXTRACTION AND MASK-BASED
VECTORIZATION
Building extraction from high-resolution aerial and satellite
images has been widely studied because of its importance
in urban mapping, disaster assessment, land management,
digital-twin construction, and geographic information sys-
tems (GIS). Early deep-learning-based methods mainly
formulated building extraction as a semantic or instance seg-
mentation task. Representative architectures, such as FCN,
U-Net, Mask R-CNN, DeepLabv3+, and dense semantic
labeling networks, have achieved strong performance in
extracting building regions or footprints from remote-sensing
imagery [9], [10], [11], [12], [13].
Although mask-based methods are effective for region-
level building extraction, their outputs are usually raster
masks or boundary probability maps. Such representations
are not directly equivalent to vectorized building models
because they do not explicitly provide ordered vertices,
edge adjacency, or graph topology. In practical CAD/GIS-
oriented workflows, raster masks must be converted into
polygons through contour tracing, boundary simplification,
geometric regularization, and topology repair. These post-
processing steps may introduce geometric errors, especially
in complex rooftop scenes with occlusions, weak textures,
non-Manhattan layouts, repetitive roof patterns, shadows, and
small roof junctions.
Recent building vectorization studies have attempted to
reduce this raster-to-vector gap [4] by predicting polygonal
contours, frame fields, graph connections, or learnable
building contours directly from remote-sensing imagery [4],
[5], [14], [15], [16]. However, many of these methods
mainly focus on building footprints or outline polygons. For
LoD2-oriented roof modeling, structured building vector-
ization further requires reliable roof-level corners, internal
structural edges, and graph-oriented primitive reasoning.
Therefore, building vectorization requires not only accurate
region extraction but also reliable corner–edge structural
primitive reasoning.
B. CORNER, LINE, AND WIREFRAME DETECTION
Corner, line, and wireframe detection methods provide
more structured geometric representations than dense seg-
mentation. Traditional handcrafted line detectors are com-
putationally efficient, but their performance can degrade
under shadows, low contrast, weak texture, and clut-
tered backgrounds. Recent learning-based methods improve
robustness by directly learning geometric primitives from
data. L-CNN detects junctions and verifies candidate line
segments using a line-of-interest pooling mechanism [17].
HAWP represents line segments through attraction fields
and improves structural line grouping [18]. LETR formu-
lates line segment detection as a transformer-based set
prediction task and avoids dependence on conventional edge
maps [19]. More recent line segment detectors, such as
M-LSD [20] and DeepLSD [21], further explore efficient
line detection and geometric refinement, indicating that
accurate structural primitive extraction remains important for
downstream geometry-aware vision tasks. These methods
have significantly advanced generic wireframe parsing and
line segment detection.
However, architectural building vectorization imposes
stricter structural requirements than generic line detection.
It is not sufficient to detect visually salient line segments
independently. The detected primitives should support valid
vertex connectivity, stable edge adjacency, and downstream
polygon formation. In roof structure reconstruction, missed
corners may remove all incident edges from the graph, while
redundant or incorrectly connected edges may lead to invalid
cycles. Therefore, remote-sensing building vectorization
requires a corner–edge reasoning framework that preserves
both local vertex accuracy and global structural consistency.
C. STRUCTURED BUILDING RECONSTRUCTION
Structured building reconstruction aims to infer compact
geometric representations such as corners, edges, planar
graphs, and polygons from images. Compared with mask-
based extraction, structured reconstruction methods [6],
[7], [8], [14] are more suitable for downstream editing,
CAD/GIS integration, and LoD-oriented building modeling.
The CityGML standard and LoD-related studies also empha-
size that roof structures, semantic surfaces, and geometric
details are important for practical 3D city modeling and GIS
applications [1], [2]. In remote-sensing-based 3D building
vectorization, Wang et al. further showed that roof edges, roof
corners, and polygonal representations are important interme-
diate structures for LoD2-oriented building modeling [3].
Nauata et al. proposed structured outdoor architecture
reconstruction by exploration and classification, showing
the importance of explicitly modeling building structure
rather than relying only on raster outputs [22]. Conv-MPN
further formulates outdoor architecture reconstruction as
convolutional message passing over geometric primitives,
allowing candidate edges to be refined through contextual
graph reasoning [6]. HEA T introduces holistic edge attention
and jointly reasons about corners and edges using transformer
decoders, improving structured reconstruction in complex
architectural scenes [7]. CornerFormer enhances corner
representation by transferring fine-grained corner features
to edge prediction and introducing direction-aware corner
proposals for adjacent corners and small edges [8].
These methods demonstrate the effectiveness of explicit
corner–edge modeling. Nevertheless, message-passing-based
or transformer-heavy designs may introduce increased
computational demands when processing large-scale high-
resolution remote-sensing imagery. In addition, polygon
validity can still be affected by missed keypoints, unstable
edge connectivity, unordered vertices, or post-processing-
dependent graph formation. These limitations motivate an
efficient framework that preserves the benefits of explicit
corner–edge reasoning while reducing computational burden
and improving structural primitive quality.

D. EFFICIENT MUL TI-SCALE AND ATTENTION-BASED
FEATURE LEARNING
High-resolution urban imagery contains buildings with
different scales, roof structures of varying complexity, and
many sparse or densely distributed junctions. Multi-scale
feature learning is therefore important for extracting both
small roof corners and large structural components. Feature
Pyramid Networks and path aggregation structures combine
high-level semantic information with low-level spatial details
and have been widely used in detection and segmentation
tasks [23], [24]. However, a single top-down fusion pathway
may weaken shallow geometric cues that are crucial for
precise corner localization and edge connectivity.
Compact backbones and attention mechanisms provide
another way to improve the accuracy-efficiency trade-off.
CSPNet reduces redundant gradient flow and improves
computational efficiency while preserving representation
capability [25]. Swin Transformer introduces shifted-window
self-attention, which captures local and cross-window con-
text with lower complexity than global self-attention [26].
Channel attention and spatial attention modules, such as
SE and CBAM, adaptively emphasize informative feature
channels and discriminative spatial regions [27], [28]. These
mechanisms are useful for suppressing background clutter,
weak roof textures, shadow-induced ambiguity, and irrelevant
region responses.
In the proposed CornerDYO framework, multi-scale fusion
and attention refinement are not used only for generic
region saliency enhancement. Instead, they are embedded
into a corner–edge reasoning pipeline. The additional high-
resolution feature level is designed to provide fine-grained
cues for small roof junctions; the two-way feature pyramid
strengthens the interaction between semantic context and
shallow geometric cues, and hybrid channel-spatial attention
refines corner-discriminative features before edge adjacency
inference.
E. RESEARCH GAP
Existing studies have improved building extraction, line
detection, building polygon extraction, and structured recon-
struction, but several gaps remain for remote-sensing building
vectorization. First, segmentation-based methods mainly
produce raster masks or boundary maps and do not directly
output ordered corner–edge primitives. Second, polygonal
building extraction methods improve vectorized footprint
generation, but many of them mainly focus on outline poly-
gons rather than roof-level corner–edge structural primitives
for LoD2-oriented vectorization [3], [4], [5], [14], [15].
Third, generic line and wireframe detectors can identify
geometric elements, but they do not necessarily enforce roof-
level connectivity, polygon closure, or graph validity. Fourth,
structured reconstruction methods based on message passing
or transformer reasoning can improve graph-level repre-
sentation, but they may introduce increased computational
demands and still depend on post-processing for polygon
formation. Fifth, generic multi-scale and attention modules
are often optimized for region-level recognition or visual
saliency rather than vertex-level structural reasoning.
To address these gaps, this article proposes CornerDYO,
an efficient corner–edge reasoning framework for struc-
tured building vectorization from remote-sensing images.
CornerDYO explicitly predicts corner confidence and edge
adjacency, integrates high-resolution multi-scale feature
extension, two-way feature pyramid fusion, context refine-
ment, and hybrid channel–spatial attention, and serves as
a structural primitive extraction front-end for downstream
planar graph construction and polygon-oriented building
vectorization.
III. METHODOLOGY
A. OVERVIEW OF CornerDYO
CornerDYO is designed as an efficient corner–edge structural
primitive extraction framework for building vectorization
from high-resolution remote-sensing images. Given an RGB
aerial image patch I ∈ RH ×W ×3, the model predicts two
coupled structural outputs: a corner confidence map Pc and
an edge adjacency representation Ae. Candidate vertices are
selected from Pc, while valid corner-to-corner connections
are inferred from Ae. The resulting corner–edge primitives
are then assembled into a planar graph, which serves as a
compact front-end representation for downstream polygon-
oriented building vectorization.
The overall formulation of CornerDYO is defined as
follows:

where B(·) denotes the CSPDarknet-Light backbone, Ftw(·)
denotes the two-way feature pyramid fusion module, A(·)
represents the context and hybrid attention refinement
module, and Hc(·) and He(·) denote the corner prediction head

The detected corners and edges are represented as a planar

cycle extraction, and polygon closure constraints to obtain
ordered structural primitives for downstream CAD/GIS-
compatible vectorization. In this article, the main objective
is to improve the accuracy and connectivity of corner–edge
primitives rather than to claim complete polygon-level
reconstruction superiority.

B. CSPDarknet-LIGHT BACKBONE AND SWIN CONTEXT
REFINEMENT
The backbone of CornerDYO is based on a compact
CSPDarknet architecture. The Cross Stage Partial design
reduces redundant gradient flow while retaining sufficient
representation capability for fine-grained corner localization.
To further reduce computational complexity, selected channel
widths are compressed using 1 × 1 bottleneck convolutions,
and efficient convolutional blocks are used for feature
extraction and fusion.
Architectural roof structures often contain long ridges,
repetitive roof patterns, weak-texture regions, shadows,
and partially occluded boundaries. Local convolutional
features alone may be insufficient to distinguish true roof
junctions from texture-induced responses or background
clutter. To strengthen contextual reasoning without using
a global transformer backbone, a localized Swin-based
context refinement block is inserted after the spatial pyra-
mid feature aggregation stage. Window-based self-attention
captures local contextual dependencies with manageable
computational cost, while shifted-window attention supports
cross-window interaction near building boundaries and roof
intersections.
The Swin block is used as a context refinement component
rather than as a heavy global transformer backbone. There-
fore, CornerDYO retains convolutional feature extraction as
the main computational pathway while introducing localized
contextual reasoning for complex rooftop scenes.
C. MUL TI-SCALE FEATURE EXTENSION AND TWO-WA Y
FEATURE PYRAMID FUSION
Roof corners are sparse, local, and highly sensitive to image
resolution. Small roof junctions or short structural edges
may disappear after repeated downsampling in conventional
convolutional backbones. To improve sensitivity to such
fine-grained structures, CornerDYO introduces an additional
high-resolution feature level. Specifically, the backbone
produces three feature maps:
F52 ∈ R52×52×128,
F26 ∈ R26×26×256,
F13 ∈ R13×13×512. (8)
The high-resolution feature map F52 provides fine-grained
spatial cues for small corners and local boundaries, while
F26 and F13 provide stronger semantic context for larger roof
structures.
Instead of using a conventional one-way feature pyramid,
CornerDYO adopts a two-way feature pyramid fusion
strategy. The top-down path propagates high-level seman-
tic information to higher-resolution features, whereas the
bottom-up path reinforces shallow geometric cues and
improves structural consistency. This bidirectional interac-
tion reduces the loss of edge and junction details during
feature fusion.

channel–spatial attention mechanism.

E. CORNER CONFIDENCE, EDGE ADJACENCY, AND
GRAPH-ORIENTED DECODING
Based on the attention-refined feature map Fatt, CornerDYO
uses two prediction heads. The corner branch predicts a
confidence map Pc, which indicates the probability of each
location being a valid roof vertex. The edge branch predicts
an adjacency representation Ae, which estimates whether two
candidate corners should be structurally connected.
Candidate corners are first selected by confidence thresh-
olding and local non-maximum suppression:
V = {vi | Pc(vi) ≥ τc, vi ∈ NMS(Pc)}. (17)
After confidence thresholding and NMS, at most 150 cor-
ner candidates are retained according to their confidence
scores, consistent with the HEA T outdoor benchmark
setting. Following the HEA T edge-candidate formulation,
all unordered pairs among the retained corners are initially
considered as candidate edges. For N retained corners, this
yields at most N (N − 1)/2 initial pairs (11,175 pairs when
N = 150). A candidate-filtering stage scores these pairs and
retains at most 3N candidates for the subsequent relational
edge decoding, thereby reducing the number of candidates
passed to the more expensive decoding stage. During
training, edge candidates are supervised by their ground-truth
adjacency labels using the masked edge-learning strategy
adopted in HEA T.

The resulting planar graph G = (V , E) provides a compact

making it more suitable for downstream roof vectorization
and CAD/GIS editing. In practice, redundant or invalid edges
can be removed by graph pruning, and closed cycles can
be extracted to form ordered structural primitives. Since the
current evaluation focuses on corner and edge quality, graph-
based polygon formation is treated as a downstream extension
rather than the main evaluated target of this article.

IV. EXPERIMENTS
A. DATASET AND EVALUATION PROTOCOL
Experiments were conducted on the Outdoor Architecture
Reconstruction benchmark used by Conv-MPN, HEA T,
and CornerFormer [6], [7], [8]. The dataset contains
2,001 satellite RGB building patches collected from Paris,
Las V egas, and Atlanta. Following the standard HEA T split,
1,601 images were used for training, 50 images for validation,
and 350 images for testing.
This benchmark is challenging because the satellite images
include off-nadir views, foreshortening, shadows, vegetation
occlusion, repetitive roof patterns, weak roof texture, and
non-Manhattan building layouts. These conditions make
the dataset suitable for evaluating whether a model can
recover both local structural primitives and graph-oriented
roof topology.
Two comparison protocols are reported. First, a controlled
comparison with HEA T was conducted under the same
data split, optimizer setting, input resolution, and fixed
500-epoch training schedule. This comparison is used as
the primary evidence for evaluating the effectiveness and
training efficiency of CornerDYO. Second, a literature-
level comparison is included using publicly reported results
of IP , Conv-MPN, Exp-Cls, HAWP , LETR, HEA T, and
CornerFormer on the same benchmark. These published
results broaden the experimental context, but they should be
interpreted as reference baselines because training schedules,
hardware platforms, and implementation details may differ
across papers.
The main evaluation metrics are precision, recall, and
F1-score for corner and edge primitives. Following the bench-
mark evaluation protocol, predicted corners are matched
to ground-truth corners under the standard distance toler-
ance, with duplicate matches counted as false positives.
Edge correctness is evaluated according to the predicted
structural connectivity between matched corner pairs. For
consistency with the HEA T evaluation protocol, true-
positive, false-positive, and ground-truth primitive counts
are accumulated over the entire test set to compute dataset-
level precision and recall. The F1-score is subsequently
calculated as the harmonic mean of precision and recall,
i.e., F1 = 2PR/(P + R). The same matching criteria,
evaluation procedure, and threshold settings are applied to
HEA T and CornerDYO in the controlled comparison.
Since the current implementation focuses on corner–edge
primitive extraction, region-level and polygon-level metrics
are treated as downstream evaluation targets rather than as
the primary objective of this article.
B. IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
CornerDYO was implemented in PyTorch and trained using
the Adam optimizer. The initial learning rate was set to
2 × 10−4, and the weight decay was set to 1 × 10−5.
Unless otherwise specified, all controlled experiments used
a fixed 500-epoch training schedule, following the HEA T
training protocol. Experiments were conducted at two input
resolutions, 256 × 256 and 512 × 512.
The corner loss weight was set to 0.05, while the edge-
related binary cross-entropy loss weight was set to 1.0.
These weights were selected according to validation-set
performance to balance sparse corner supervision and edge-
adjacency learning. The same loss weights were used for all
CornerDYO experiments.
All controlled experiments were conducted on a worksta-
tion equipped with an Intel Xeon-class CPU at 3.4 GHz,
24 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA RTX A5000 GPU with 24 GB
memory. The software environment consisted of Win-
dows 10 Professional 64-bit, Python 3.9, PyTorch 2.2.1, and
CUDA 12.3. Both HEA T and CornerDYO were trained under
the same hardware and software environment, optimizer
setting, input resolution, and 500-epoch training schedule.
No early stopping was used; every controlled run completed
the full 500-epoch schedule.
The reported training time refers to the total wall-clock
time required to train each model for 500 epochs under the
controlled setting. Accordingly, the efficiency claim in this
study is restricted to training wall-clock time under the same
experimental environment and should not be interpreted as
a complete comparison of model size, FLOPs, peak GPU
memory consumption, or per-image inference latency.
C. COMPARISON WITH HEAT UNDER CONTROLLED
SETTINGS
Table 1 reports the controlled corner detection comparison
between HEA T and CornerDYO. At 256 × 256 reso-
lution, CornerDYO improves the corner F1-score from
87.1% to 90.3%. At 512 × 512 resolution, the improvement
becomes more pronounced, increasing from 88.7% to 96.7%.
These results indicate that the proposed multi-scale feature
enhancement and bidirectional feature fusion contribute
to improved sensitivity to fine-grained roof-junction cues,
particularly when richer spatial details are available.
Table 2 reports the controlled edge detection comparison.
At 512 × 512 resolution, CornerDYO improves the edge
F1-score from 79.7% to 83.8%. The recall gain is particularly
important because missed edges may interrupt polygon cycles
and produce incomplete roof structures. These results show
that CornerDYO improves not only vertex-level localization
but also edge-level structural connectivity.

should be interpreted as a progressive component-integration

results therefore support the effectiveness of the integrated
design while avoiding attribution of the final performance

G. FAILURE CASES AND LIMITATIONS
Despite the improved corner and edge F1-scores, several
failure cases remain. Figure 4 presents representative failure
examples under challenging remote-sensing conditions.
Severe occlusion caused by trees, shadows, or adjacent
objects may hide roof junctions and lead to missed corner
predictions.
A further limitation is that the current experimental
evidence is strongest at the corner and edge levels.
Since structured building vectorization ultimately depends
on valid roof polygons, higher primitive-level scores do
not necessarily guarantee valid polygon closure, graph
connectivity, or region-level consistency. Accordingly, the
present study does not claim complete polygon-level
reconstruction superiority. A comprehensive evaluation
of polygon-level geometric accuracy, topology validity,
and reconstruction completeness remains an important
direction for future work. Although the benchmark
includes imagery from Paris, Las V egas, and Atlanta,
the present evaluation follows the standard aggregated
test split and does not establish cross-dataset geo-
graphic generalization. V alidation on additional exter-
nal datasets remains an important direction for future
work.

V. CONCLUSION
This article presented CornerDYO, an efficient corner–edge
reasoning framework for structured building vectorization
from remote-sensing images. The proposed framework
integrates high-resolution feature enhancement, bidirec-
tional multi-scale fusion, context refinement, and hybrid
channel–spatial attention to improve building-corner local-
ization and edge-connectivity prediction.
Under the controlled comparison with HEA T, CornerDYO
achieves improved corner and edge detection performance
while reducing training wall-clock time under the same
experimental setting. The results demonstrate the effective-
ness of combining fine-grained geometric feature enhance-
ment with explicit corner–edge reasoning for structural
primitive extraction. The literature-level comparisons further
provide contextual evidence of the competitiveness of the
proposed framework on the Outdoor Architecture Recon-
struction benchmark.
Although CornerDYO improves corner localization and
edge connectivity, its performance can still be affected by
severe occlusions, weak visual textures, and ambiguous roof
structures. The present study focuses primarily on corner-
and edge-level structural primitives and does not claim
complete polygon-level reconstruction superiority. Future
work will investigate more robust topology-aware learning
strategies and extend the framework toward larger-scale
building vectorization and topology-aware reconstruction.
